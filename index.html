The Electrocardiogram (ECG)

What it is, how it works, where it is used today, and where it is heading

1. What is it? 2. How it works 3. Indications today 4. Future indications 5. Limitations References

1. What is an ECG?

The electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) is a non-invasive recording of the heart's electrical activity over time, made with electrodes placed on the skin.[1] It is one of the most frequently performed tests in medicine: quick, inexpensive, painless and without radiation. It shows electrical events, not the pumping force or structure of the heart directly, but many structural and metabolic problems leave an electrical "signature".

The standard test is the 12-lead ECG (10 electrodes: 4 limb, 6 chest), which views the heart from 12 different "angles". Single-lead and few-lead versions exist for monitors, patches and wearables.

2. How does it work?

Physiological basis

Each heartbeat starts in the sinoatrial (SA) node. The impulse spreads through the atria, is delayed in the AV node, then travels via the His bundle, bundle branches and Purkinje fibres to depolarise the ventricles. Cardiac muscle cells change their membrane voltage (depolarisation, then repolarisation) through ion flow (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, K⁺). The sum of these voltage changes creates electrical fields that spread through the body and can be picked up at the skin surface.[1]

Measurement principle

  • Each lead measures the voltage difference between two points (or between one electrode and a reference).
  • An electrical wave moving towards a lead's positive pole gives an upward deflection; moving away gives a downward deflection.
  • The signal is amplified and filtered, then drawn on paper or screen, typically at 25 mm/s and 10 mm/mV.

Systematic interpretation

Rate → rhythm → axis → intervals (PR, QRS, QT) → morphology of P, QRS, ST, T → comparison with a prior ECG and, always, the clinical context.[1]

Common ECG formats

  • Resting 12-lead ECG – snapshot of ~10 seconds.
  • Exercise ECG (stress test) – ECG recorded under increasing workload.[5]
  • Holter / event recorder / patch / implantable loop recorder – prolonged rhythm monitoring (days to years).
  • Telemetry / bedside monitoring – continuous in hospital.
  • Wearables – smartwatch single-lead ECG, handheld devices.

3. Established clinical indications today

Overview of the main uses; detailed class-of-recommendation levels are in the cited guidelines.

Acute chest pain / suspected acute coronary syndrome

A 12-lead ECG is recommended within about 10 minutes of first medical contact. ST-elevation (STEMI) or equivalents trigger immediate reperfusion therapy; ST depression/T-wave changes support NSTE-ACS.[3][4][7]

Arrhythmias

Diagnosis of atrial fibrillation/flutter, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, bradyarrhythmias and AV blocks. ECG documentation is essential to confirm AF.[2][8]

Syncope, palpitations, dizziness

Looking for conduction disease, pre-excitation, long-QT, Brugada pattern, or documenting rhythm during symptoms with ambulatory monitoring.[1][9]

Inherited arrhythmia syndromes & sudden-death risk

Long-QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, WPW, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy and family screening after sudden cardiac death.[6][9]

Structural heart disease & heart failure

Hints of left ventricular hypertrophy, prior infarction, cardiomyopathy, pericarditis, pulmonary embolism (strain pattern) and atrial enlargement. Normally a trigger for echocardiography, which is the confirming test.[1]

Metabolic & drug effects

Hyperkalaemia/hypokalaemia, calcium disorders, and monitoring of QT-prolonging drugs (e.g. some antiarrhythmics, antipsychotics, macrolides).[1][10]

Stress testing & risk assessment

Exercise ECG for suspected ischaemia, exercise-induced arrhythmias, and functional capacity; baseline ECG before many surgeries, sports pre-participation screening (in some countries) and cardiotoxic therapy monitoring.[5]

Device & therapy follow-up

Pacemaker and ICD function, cardiac resynchronisation (QRS width), response to antiarrhythmic drugs and post-ablation follow-up.

A normal ECG does not exclude disease (e.g. early ACS, intermittent paroxysmal arrhythmias), and an abnormal ECG is not always disease. Always interpret it in clinical context.

4. Future indications currently under development

The ECG's hardware is old (over 100 years), but its information content is being re-mined using deep learning and consumer wearables. Many of these applications are at different stages of maturity: some are cleared or in pragmatic trials, others remain research-only.

AI-ECG: detecting hidden structural heart disease

Neural networks trained on millions of ECGs can detect left ventricular systolic dysfunction (low ejection fraction) from a standard 12-lead ECG. In the pragmatic cluster-randomised EAGLE trial in primary care, AI-guided ECG screening increased diagnosis of low EF.[11][12] Similar models are being evaluated for cardiomyopathies (hypertrophic, amyloid), valvular disease and left-ventricular hypertrophy.[13]

Most advanced: pragmatic RCT evidence
AI-ECG: predicting atrial fibrillation from sinus rhythm

An AI model applied to a normal sinus-rhythm ECG can estimate the probability that a patient has had or will develop AF, potentially guiding targeted monitoring (e.g. after cryptogenic stroke).[14]

Prospective outcome trials ongoing/needed
Wearable & smartphone ECG for screening and monitoring

Smartwatches with irregular-pulse notification and single-lead ECG can identify possible AF in large populations. In the Apple Heart Study, notified participants had AF confirmed on subsequent ECG patch monitoring in a substantial proportion, but overall AF detection rates were low and the clinical benefit of population screening is still being studied.[15] Guidelines currently discuss opportunistic or targeted screening and the role of consumer devices.[8] Further targets: continuous rhythm monitoring after ablation, QT monitoring from home, and remote heart-failure care.

Evolving: screening benefit uncertain
Deep-learning arrhythmia classification

Algorithms trained on single-lead ambulatory recordings have shown cardiologist-level classification of many rhythm types, which could scale up interpretation of long-term monitoring.[16]

Approaching clinical use (decision support)
Non-cardiac & systemic conditions from the ECG

Research explores detecting hyperkalaemia (including from a single-lead smartwatch-type signal), age/sex and "biological age", diabetes or anaemia signals, and estimating future cardiovascular risk from the ECG.[17] Most are research-stage and need external validation.

Research stage
Acute coronary occlusion beyond classic STEMI criteria

A sizeable share of NSTEMI patients have an acutely occluded artery without classical ST elevation ("occlusion MI"). Refined ECG criteria and AI tools are being developed to identify these earlier; the 2023 ESC ACS guidelines already discuss ECG patterns that suggest ongoing occlusion.[7]

Under investigation
Advanced hardware: ECG imaging, patches, and smart textiles

Non-invasive electrocardiographic imaging (ECGI) maps the heart's surface electrical activity to guide ablation; adhesive patches and textile electrodes enable long-term, comfortable monitoring; paper ECG photos can be digitised and analysed by AI.

Mixed maturity
Key open questions: external validation across populations, bias and fairness, regulatory clearance, workflow integration and, above all, proof that acting on AI-ECG findings improves patient outcomes.

5. Limitations

  • Limited sensitivity for some conditions (e.g. early ACS, paroxysmal arrhythmias between episodes).
  • Artefacts (movement, electrode misplacement, lead reversal) can mimic disease.
  • Automated computer interpretation is error-prone and needs physician over-reading.
  • Consumer/AI tools generate false positives, which can cause anxiety and extra testing.
